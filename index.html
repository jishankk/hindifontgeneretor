What makes Hindi text look right in a font

Most Devanagari letters hang from a horizontal line called the shirorekha. In a well-made font that line runs unbroken from one letter to the next, so a word reads as a single strip. If you see small gaps in it, the cause is usually extra letter-spacing added in a design app. Leave letter-spacing at zero for Hindi.

Matras (vowel signs) sit above, below or beside the letter, as in कि, की, कु, कू, के and कै. They need vertical room, so a tight line height can make them touch the line above. For text on more than one line, about 1.5 times the font size is a safe line height.

Conjuncts such as क्ष, त्र, ज्ञ and श्र are single shapes built from two or more letters. Most modern fonts draw them well, but some decorative fonts stack them awkwardly or show a visible halant (्). That is the main reason to test your own phrase instead of judging a font by one letter.

Thin strokes also do not survive shrinking. Small marks like the chandrabindu (ँ) and the nukta (the dot in ड़ or क़) are the first to vanish, so pick a medium or bold weight for anything people will read on a phone.

Unicode Hindi fonts vs. old font-based text

Modern Hindi typing uses Unicode Devanagari. A word like नमस्ते is stored as standard characters that can be searched, copied and read by any website or app. The font only changes how those characters look.

Older legacy workflows (such as KrutiDev) depend on a special character mapping, so changing the font alone does not convert them. If you have legacy text, use the Unicode ⇄ KrutiDev converter. To find a specific symbol or matra, try the Hindi character map.

Common problems and quick fixes

  • Boxes instead of letters: the font is still loading or does not contain that character. Wait a second, then try another style.
  • A card says the preview is unavailable: the font request failed, usually because of a weak connection or a blocker. Reload the page or try again in a moment.

  • A dot floating beside ड़, ढ़ or क़: some keyboards type the nukta as a separate character and not every font joins it neatly. Retype the letter or try a different font.

Devanagari in brief: the script behind Hindi fonts

Devanagari is an abugida: a writing system where each consonant carries an inherent vowel sound, and other vowels are added with marks called matras. It is written left to right and descends from the ancient Brahmi script through the Nagari scripts of medieval India. Besides Hindi, it is used for Marathi, Nepali, Sanskrit, Konkani, Maithili, Bodo and many other languages. Hindi written in Devanagari is the official language of the Union of India under Article 343 of the Constitution.

For type designers this makes Devanagari far more demanding than Latin. A single font must draw consonants, independent vowels, matras, numerals and punctuation, and also the many conjunct forms where two or more consonants fuse into one shape. In the days of metal type this meant a very large case of pieces. Digital fonts solve it with OpenType layout features, rules that tell the software when to swap letters for a conjunct, when to move a reph (the small hook that stands for a leading र्, as in कर्म) above the next letter, and how to position matras.

In Unicode, the main Devanagari block runs from U+0900 to U+097F. Every character you type here, from अ to the halant ्, comes from that block. Good fonts do not change which characters you typed; they only change how the shaping engine draws them. This is why two fonts can show the same word with different conjunct shapes while the stored text stays identical.

The fonts in this generator come from Google Fonts, whose Devanagari collection has grown considerably over the past decade. It now covers everything from newspaper-style serifs and plain interface sans-serifs to brush-style display faces and handwriting, which is the reason a side-by-side comparison is worth doing before you commit to a design.

Colour combinations that keep Hindi text readable

Devanagari has fine details above and below the line, so low contrast hurts it more than it hurts plain Latin text. The Web Content Accessibility Guidelines ask for a contrast ratio of at least 4.5:1 for normal text and 3:1 for large text. Even for a poster that nobody will audit, those numbers are a useful target. The pairs below are easy to read; you can paste the hex codes into the colour pickers above.

Pairs to avoid: red on green, yellow on white, light grey on white and any bright colour on a busy photo without an outline or shadow. If you must put text on a photo, a darker overlay behind the text is more reliable than any effect.

Seven common mistakes with Hindi typography

  1. Adding letter-spacing. Spacing that works for English breaks the shirorekha and can pull matras away from their letters. Keep it at zero.
  2. Tight line height. Matras above and below need room. Around 1.5 to 1.8 times the font size suits Hindi paragraphs.
  3. Using faux bold or italic. If a font has no real bold or italic, the app fakes it by smearing or slanting the letters. Devanagari has no true italic tradition, so slanted Hindi usually looks wrong. Choose a family that ships a real bold weight instead.
  4. Justified text on narrow columns. Hindi words are often long, and justification on a phone-width column opens ugly gaps. Left-align short columns.
  5. Mixing too many fonts. One display font for the headline and one plain font for the body is enough. A third font rarely helps.
  6. Choosing thin hairline fonts for small sizes. The nukta and chandrabindu disappear. Use a medium or bold weight below about 18px.
  7. Trusting a single test word. Test the exact phrase, including its conjuncts and punctuation. The generator exists to make that easy.

How to type Hindi on phone, Windows and Mac

You do not need a special keyboard to use this tool. Any way of producing Unicode Hindi will work, and these are the most common.

  • Android (Gboard): open Settings, then Languages and input, add Hindi, and choose the transliteration layout if you want to type "namaste" and get नमस्ते. The handwriting and the standard Hindi layouts are also available.
  • iPhone: go to Settings, General, Keyboard, Keyboards, Add New Keyboard, then pick Hindi and either the Transliteration or the Devanagari layout.
  • Windows: add Hindi under Time and language, Language and region. Then switch between layouts with the Windows key plus Space. Hindi Traditional and the phonetic options both produce Unicode text.
  • Mac: add Hindi in System Settings under Keyboard, Input Sources. The default shortcut for switching input sources is Control plus Space.
  • Browser-based typing tools: many free transliteration websites let you type phonetically and copy the Hindi result, which you can paste into the box at the top of this page.

If a word will not come out right, the Hindi character map lets you click individual letters, matras and symbols, which is handy for rare conjuncts and the nukta.

Using Hindi fonts on your own website

If you build websites, the same Google Fonts you preview here can be loaded on your own pages. Here is a minimal, practical setup.

<link rel="preconnect" href="https://fonts.googleapis.com">
<link rel="preconnect" href="https://fonts.gstatic.com" crossorigin>
<link href="https://fonts.googleapis.com/css2?family=Hind:wght@400;600&display=swap" rel="stylesheet">

<style>
  html:lang(hi) body {
    font-family: "Hind", "Noto Sans Devanagari", system-ui, sans-serif;
    line-height: 1.7;      /* room for matras */
    letter-spacing: 0;     /* never add spacing to Devanagari */
  }
</style>
  • Declare the language. Put lang="hi" on the page or on the element. It helps browsers choose the right fallback font and helps screen readers pronounce the text correctly.
  • Always add a fallback. List a second Devanagari font after your first choice, so text stays readable if the first font fails to load.
  • Use display=swap. The page shows text in a fallback font at once and swaps when the real font arrives, so visitors are never staring at blank space.
  • Load only the weights you use. Each extra weight is another download. Two weights, regular and bold, cover most designs.
  • Check licences. The fonts listed here are open-licensed on Google Fonts, but you should still read the licence on a font's page before shipping it in a commercial product.

Hindi typography glossary

Shirorekha (शिरोरेखा)
The horizontal line along the top of most Devanagari letters. In good fonts it joins neighbouring letters into one continuous bar.
Matra (मात्रा)
A vowel sign attached to a consonant, such as the ा in का or the ि in कि. The short ि is written before the consonant but pronounced after it, which is a classic test of font shaping.
Halant or virama (हलन्त)
The mark ् that removes a consonant's inherent vowel. It is what joins consonants into conjuncts.
Conjunct (संयुक्ताक्षर)
A combined letter form built from two or more consonants, for example क्ष, त्र and ज्ञ.
Reph
The superscript form of र when it comes before another consonant, as in धर्म.
Nukta (नुक्ता)
The dot placed under a letter to make sounds such as क़, ख़ or ज़.
Chandrabindu and anusvara
The nasal marks ँ and ं. Thin fonts can make them hard to see at small sizes.
Visarga
The two-dot mark ः, common in Sanskrit words such as नमः.
Glyph
The drawn shape for a character or combination of characters in a given font. One Unicode character can have several glyphs.
OpenType shaping
The set of rules inside a font and the software that applies them, which decides how typed characters turn into the final glyphs on screen.
Unicode
The international standard that gives every character a unique code, so the same Hindi text works across devices, apps and fonts.
